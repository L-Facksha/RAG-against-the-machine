A plain-language walkthrough

How RAG works

Retrieval-Augmented Generation is how an AI model answers questions using facts it looks up first, instead of only relying on what it memorized during training. Here's the idea, step by step — with a working example at the end.

Your question → Look things up → Write the answer

The problem RAG solves

A language model's knowledge is frozen at training time, and it doesn't know anything private to you — your company's policies, yesterday's news, your own documents. Asked about those things, it can only guess. RAG gives it a way to check first.

Without RAG — closed book

The model answers purely from memory. If the answer isn't in what it learned during training, it may guess fluently and confidently — and be wrong. This is where "hallucination" comes from.

With RAG — open book

Before answering, the model retrieves relevant text from a trusted source — documents, a database, a website — and is told to base its answer on that. It's an exam where it's allowed to check its notes.

How this connects to a real LLM

The demo above matches on shared words so you can see the mechanism. A production system does the same four steps, but retrieval works on meaning, and "augmenting the prompt" is a literal API call — nothing mystical.

1 · Split documents into chunks

A whole document is usually too long and covers too many topics to embed as one piece, so it first gets cut into smaller chunks — often 200–500 words each. Consecutive chunks usually overlap by a sentence or two, so a fact sitting right on the boundary doesn't get orphaned in a chunk that no longer makes sense on its own. Here's a longer café FAQ paragraph split into two overlapping chunks — the shared sentence is highlighted in both:

CHUNK 1

Fern & Kettle sources its coffee from small Ethiopian and Colombian farms and roasts beans in-house every Thursday. All pastries are baked fresh each morning starting at 5am, and the almond croissants sell out first — usually by 10am on weekends.

CHUNK 2

...and the almond croissants sell out first — usually by 10am on weekends. Because we bake with almond and oat flour blends for several items, guests with tree nut allergies should ask staff before ordering, since cross-contact is possible even in nut-free items.

Without that overlap, Chunk 2 on its own would start mid-thought — "…and the almond croissants sell out first" — with no idea what it's referring to. The repeated sentence keeps each chunk understandable by itself.

2 · Turn each chunk into a vector

An embedding model reads a chunk and outputs a fixed-length list of numbers — real ones typically have 1,024 to 3,072 numbers per chunk, and no single number means anything on its own. What matters is that chunks about similar things end up as numbers that point in similar directions. To make that visible, here's the same idea with just 3 made-up dimensions instead of thousands — real embeddings don't have labeled dimensions like this, it's purely to build intuition:

Chunk food/allergen plant/garden rewards
Menu & Allergens 0.90 0.05 0.02
Plant Care & Returns 0.05 0.92 0.03
Loyalty Program 0.04 0.02 0.94
Your question: "Do your pastries have nuts in them?" 0.88 0.04 0.01

Notice the question's vector [0.88, 0.04, 0.01] sits almost exactly where the "Menu & Allergens" chunk sits, and nowhere near the plant or rewards chunks — even though the question never uses the words "menu" or "allergen."

3 · Compare vectors with cosine similarity

Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors and ignores their length — it answers "do these two point in the same direction?" A score of 1.0 means identical direction (same meaning), 0 means unrelated, -1 means opposite. The formula is the dot product of the two vectors, divided by the product of their lengths:

cos(θ) = (Q · C) / (|Q| × |C|) Question vector Q = [0.88, 0.04, 0.01] Chunk A — Menu & Allergens = [0.90, 0.05, 0.02] Chunk B — Plant Care = [0.05, 0.92, 0.03] Q · A = (0.88×0.90) + (0.04×0.05) + (0.01×0.02) = 0.794 |Q| = sqrt(0.88² + 0.04² + 0.01²) = 0.881 |A| = sqrt(0.90² + 0.05² + 0.02²) = 0.902 cos(Q, A) = 0.794 / (0.881 × 0.902) ≈ 0.9999 → almost identical direction Q · B = (0.88×0.05) + (0.04×0.92) + (0.01×0.03) = 0.081 |B| = sqrt(0.05² + 0.92² + 0.03²) = 0.922 cos(Q, B) = 0.081 / (0.881 × 0.922) ≈ 0.10 → basically unrelated

Do the same math against every chunk in the database and rank the results — the highest cosine score wins:

4 · Matching is by meaning, not shared words

Because vectors encode meaning, "Do your pastries have nuts?" can retrieve a passage that only says "tree nut allergens" — or one phrased in completely different words — as long as it's about the same thing. That's the real advantage over the keyword search used in the demo above.

5 · The retrieved text becomes part of the API call

Once the top chunks are chosen — by whichever method found them — they're inserted into the request sent to the model's API, usually in the system prompt or at the top of the user message. This is what actually gets sent to a model like Claude:

In plain terms: the system doesn't hand the model a "search result" in some special format — it just glues text together. It takes the words from the matching document chunks, pastes them in front of your question, and sends the whole thing as one ordinary block of text — the same way you'd paste a paragraph into a chat box before typing your question. The model can't tell the difference between "text a person typed" and "text a retrieval step pasted in." It just reads whatever text arrives and responds to all of it at once.

{ "model": "claude-sonnet-4-6", "system": "Answer using only the context provided below. If the answer isn't in the context, say you don't know — don't guess.", "messages": [ { "role": "user", "content": "CONTEXT:\n[Menu & Allergens] Most pastries contain gluten and tree nuts; oat and almond milk are used, no dairy on site.\n\nQUESTION:\nDo your pastries have nuts in them?" } ] }

The model has no idea a "retrieval step" happened before this. From its side, it just received one message containing some facts and a question — the same as any normal chat request. All the RAG-specific work happens before the API call, in how that message gets built.

6 · Optional: it can run more than once

Some systems let the model decide it needs to search again — read the first batch of results, realize they're not enough, and trigger another retrieval before answering. That turns RAG from a single lookup into a small loop the model can control, but the core mechanic is exactly the same three moving parts: embed, search, insert into the prompt.

Lexical vs. semantic retrieval

Everything above described semantic search — matching by meaning, via embeddings. But it isn't the only way to retrieve, and plenty of real-world RAG systems start with something simpler: matching by words.

Chunking isn't one-size-fits-all

Prose splits cleanly at sentence or paragraph boundaries — that's what the café example above did. Source code doesn't: cutting a function in half mid-body produces a chunk that means nothing on its own. So a real indexer usually needs two chunking strategies — one that respects code structure (functions, classes) and one that respects prose structure (paragraphs, headings) — chosen per file type.

Lexical retrieval: TF-IDF and BM25

Instead of embedding meaning into vectors, lexical methods score a chunk by which specific words it shares with the question — weighted so rare, distinctive words (like "espresso" or "invoice-3392") count for more than common ones (like "the" or "is"). TF-IDF does this with a straightforward frequency-times-rarity formula; BM25 is a refinement that stops a word from dominating just because it repeats many times in one chunk. Both are cheap, need no embedding model, and are excellent when questions quote exact terms — identifiers, product codes, error messages — that an embedding might blur together with similar-sounding text.

Method Matches by Best at
TF-IDF / BM25 (lexical) shared words, weighted by rarity exact terms, identifiers, quoted phrases
Embeddings + cosine similarity (semantic) meaning, regardless of wording paraphrases, synonyms, conceptual questions
Hybrid both rankings, merged covering each method's blind spots

Hybrid retrieval combines both

A hybrid system runs the lexical search and the semantic search separately, then merges the two ranked lists (often by re-scoring each chunk with a weighted mix of both scores). It catches a question that quotes an exact term and one that only paraphrases an idea — something neither method alone handles well.

Why it's worth the extra step

Fewer made-up facts

The model is grounded in real text instead of free-associating from memory.

Current information

Swap in new documents and the model's "knowledge" updates instantly — no retraining.

Private or specific data

It can answer from your company's files or a specific book without ever being trained on them.

You can check the source

Because the retrieved documents are visible, an answer can be traced back to where it came from.

Going further

Chunking, semantic search, hybrid search, and evaluation are covered above. What follows is further optimization on the same four steps — good to know exist, not required to understand RAG.

Re-ranking

A second, more precise model re-scores the top retrieved chunks to push the best ones to the front.

Metadata filtering

Narrowing the search by date, source, or permissions before similarity search runs, not after.

Incremental indexing

When one source file changes, re-index just that file instead of rebuilding the whole index from scratch.

Caching

Store the index and repeated query results so cold starts and common questions don't redo the same work.

Serving it over an API

Wrapping search and answer generation behind a small HTTP endpoint so other programs — not just a CLI — can query the system.

Multi-hop / agentic retrieval

The model searches, reads, decides it needs more, and searches again — a loop instead of one lookup.
